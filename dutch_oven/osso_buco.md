# Osso Buco (Dutch Oven)

## Ingredients

    4 veal shanks (1½–2 inches thick)  
    2 tsp kosher salt  
    1 tsp black pepper  
    ¼ cup all-purpose flour  
    3 tbsp olive oil  
    1 large onion, finely diced  
    2 carrots, finely diced  
    2 celery stalks, finely diced  
    4 cloves garlic, smashed  
    2 tbsp tomato paste  
    1 cup dry white wine  
    2 cups chicken or veal stock  
    1 bay leaf  
    2 sprigs fresh thyme  

## Optional Gremolata

    Zest of 1 lemon  
    2 tbsp fresh parsley, finely chopped  
    1 clove garlic, minced

## Tools

- Dutch oven with lid
- Tongs
- Wooden spoon
- Knife and cutting board
- Small bowl (for gremolata)
- Oven mitts

## Steps

1. Preheat oven to 300°F.
2. Pat veal shanks dry and season with salt and pepper.
3. Lightly dredge shanks in flour, shaking off excess.
4. Heat olive oil in the Dutch oven over medium-high heat.
5. Sear shanks on both sides until deeply browned. Remove to a plate.
6. Lower heat to medium. Add onion, carrot, and celery; cook until soft and lightly caramelized.
7. Add garlic and cook 30 seconds.
8. Stir in tomato paste and cook 1–2 minutes until brick red.
9. Pour in white wine and simmer 3–5 minutes, scraping up browned bits.
10. Add stock, bay leaf, and thyme.
11. Return shanks to the pot; liquid should come about halfway up the meat.
12. Cover and transfer to oven.
13. Braise for 2–2½ hours, turning shanks once, until meat is very tender and nearly falling off the bone.
14. Remove shanks and skim excess fat from sauce if needed.
15. Optional: Mix gremolata ingredients and sprinkle over shanks just before serving.
16. Rest 10 minutes before serving.

## Notes

- Veal can be substituted with beef or lamb shanks; cooking time may increase.
- Keep marrow bones intact—this is part of the dish’s richness.
- Traditionally served with risotto or polenta.

## Technique Learned

This capstone recipe teaches full braise orchestration: precise searing,
aromatic balance, controlled liquid depth, long collagen conversion, and
final contrast through acidity (gremolata). You integrate everything
learned—timing, heat, texture, and restraint—into a composed,
restaurant-level Dutch oven dish.
